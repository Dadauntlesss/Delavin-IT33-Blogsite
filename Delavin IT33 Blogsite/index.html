4.1 Exercise

October 11, 2026

When you sit in an information assurance and security class and talk about threat detection, everything sounds structured and manageable until you realize that real security operations centers are drowning in a relentless sea of noise and alert fatigue. For years, security teams have tried to plug the gaps with basic automation scripts and playbook rules, but the sheer volume of alerts makes it nearly impossible for human analysts to keep up without burning out. That is why recent discussions about shifting toward an autonomous security operations center driven by agentic artificial intelligence feel so compelling. John Velisaris from IBM suggests that security operations should take inspiration from commercial aviation where machines handle standard routine flights while human pilots step in only when something unusual happens. In this vision, analysts trade endless swivel chair investigations for the SOC pilot chair, choosing when to intervene while digital labor handles the heavy lifting. But as a third year IT student trying to wrap my head around how backend systems and security automation actually collide, I find myself wondering whether this pilot model is genuinely realistic and responsible or if it is just another shiny tech promise waiting for a messy reality check.

Where Artificial Intelligence Can Safely Act on Its Own

To evaluate whether an autonomous security center can work, we first have to understand where artificial intelligence can actually operate without human babysitting. In cybersecurity, machines can safely take the wheel only when they operate inside closed systems where every variable, asset, and endpoint is fully known and accounted for. If an organization has complete visibility over its technology stack, automated tools can execute swift responses without needing a committee meeting because the rules of engagement are crystal clear. For instance, endpoint detection and response software can automatically isolate a compromised machine if it recognizes malicious behavior and knows with absolute certainty that the device is an ordinary workstation rather than a core database server. In these controlled boundaries, AI excels at processing massive streams of telemetry, categorizing alerts, and executing predefined containment actions at machine speed. The magic ingredient that makes this safe is predictability because when the software knows the exact identity and network context of every asset it touches, automation runs smoothly and effectively without risking critical business downtime.

Where Human Judgment Must Remain Indispensable

The moment you step outside of a perfectly controlled closed system and enter the messy reality of enterprise networks, however, automation hits a massive brick wall. Most organizations rely on security information and event management systems that aggregate data from countless disparate sources where assets are constantly shifting, user behaviors fluctuate, and context is rarely straightforward. Furthermore, cybersecurity stands entirely alone in grappling with the unpredictable phenomenon of zero-day vulnerabilities which are newly discovered software flaws that the security community has never seen before. When a completely novel threat emerges from an unknown source using unfamiliar tactics, automated playbooks and rigid rule engines fall flat because they have no prior signature to match against. This is precisely where human judgment becomes irreplaceable because experienced security analysts possess the intuition, contextual reasoning, and adaptive problem solving skills required to dissect anomalies that machine learning models have never encountered. Human analysts do not just process data; they understand the broader organizational impact, weigh strategic risks, and make nuanced judgment calls that a software algorithm simply cannot replicate on its own.

The Danger of Incomplete or Erroneous Asset Information

What happens when the artificial intelligence agent itself is forced to make a decision based on incomplete or outright wrong asset data? This is where autonomous systems face their most dangerous vulnerability. In advanced frameworks like IBM's autonomous threat operations machine, the AI does not just wait for alerts to arrive; it actively gathers context by querying vulnerability management systems, configuration management databases, and endpoint tools to figure out what kind of asset it is dealing with. But what happens if the configuration database is outdated, a newly plugged in device is unlisted, or network metadata is misleading? If an autonomous agent misidentifies a critical domain controller or financial database server as an ordinary employee workstation because of a stale hostname or misconfigured network entry, the consequences can be catastrophic. An automated response designed to quarantine a workstation could inadvertently sever a core business server from the network, causing massive operational downtime and business disruption. When an AI agent operates on flawed assumptions, autonomy transforms from a productivity booster into a dangerous liability, proving that artificial intelligence is only as trustworthy as the underlying data ecosystem it relies upon.

looking at the Realism and Responsibility of the SOC Pilot Model

So, is the SOC pilot model realistic and responsible for the near future? From a technical standpoint, the progress being made with multi-agentic digital labor is undeniable, moving security operations far beyond simple if-then playbooks into genuine reasoning workflows. However, achieving true autonomy across diverse enterprise environments remains an immense challenge. Security research from firms like Gartner highlights that organizations must look beyond flashy vendor demos and evaluate whether AI agents genuinely reduce operational bottlenecks while improving critical metrics like mean time to detect and mean time to contain. More importantly, calling human analysts pilots implies a level of control and situational awareness that can easily be undermined by automation bias. If analysts spend all day watching autonomous agents handle routine investigations, they risk falling into a state of cognitive complacency where they blindly trust the machine output and fail to notice subtle indicators of compromise or algorithmic hallucinations. For the pilot model to be truly responsible, organizations must maintain rigorous governance frameworks, continuous validation, and transparent audit trails so that humans are never reduced to rubber stamping actions taken by an autonomous engine.

Reflecting on the Skills Needed to Become a SOC Pilot

As I look toward graduation and my future career in information technology and security, thinking about the SOC pilot model hits close to home because it completely redefines what entry level analysts will be expected to do. If routine alert triage, log parsing, and basic incident classification are entirely swallowed up by digital labor agents, junior professionals will no longer be able to cut their teeth on mundane grunt work. To avoid being replaced by a digital assistant and instead earn a seat in the SOC pilot chair, aspiring security professionals must cultivate a much higher tier of technical competencies. We will need deep expertise in network architecture, advanced threat hunting, and digital forensics so we can spot when an automated agent's reasoning is going off the rails. Furthermore, we must master AI governance, prompt verification, and security architecture design to ensure that we understand how these autonomous systems make decisions under the hood. Ultimately, becoming a SOC pilot means shifting from being a reactive button pusher to becoming an active strategic commander who knows how to interrogate the machine, validate its assumptions, and maintain resilient human oversight over intelligent systems.

When you spend weeks putting together a risk assessment form for a government agency as part of your college coursework you tend to look at things through a very forgiving lens because you walk into the regional office of the National Irrigation Administration and observe the legal desks and the planning rooms and chat politely with the staff and walk away feeling like you captured a comprehensive picture of their security posture and our student group evaluated ten distinct risk scenarios ranging from unlocked employee computers and server enclosures to physical case records and shared workstations where filling out impact weights and probability scores felt like a very precise and scientific exercise but if you take a step back and revisit that exact same risk assessment form through the cold and unforgiving eyes of a professional regulatory auditor the entire picture shifts dramatically

An auditor does not care how polite the employees were or how clean the office looked on a Tuesday morning and is not interested in your good intentions or your friendly rapport with the staff because an auditor looks at your risk assessment form and asks three brutal questions right off the bat asking whether this organization actually know what it owns and whether there is a clearly designated human being explicitly responsible and accountable for every single asset and whether you can show verifiable physical or digital evidence proving that your security controls actually work under pressure when you apply those questions to our fieldwork findings you realize that many of the risk treatments we accepted or recommended would not survive even five minutes in a real audit room

To understand why we have to look closely at how the organization manages its asset inventory and how it categorizes what matters most during our evaluation of the legal and planning operational areas we cataloged physical servers employee laptops shared desktop computers biometric timekeeping hardware and cabinets overflowing with confidential case records on paper these assets were acknowledged as existing within the office premises but knowing that a computer or a filing cabinet is sitting in a room is a far cry from having a formal asset inventory because a real audit requires a living configuration management database where every asset is tagged and classified by sensitivity and assigned a formal data classification level and tied directly to a specific job title or department head

In the agency we observed asset ownership was largely informal and diffused across entire units rather than assigned to specific individuals for instance when we looked at shared workstations in the planning environment the responsibility for system integrity and session locking was treated as a collective duty of the whole office rather than the explicit liability of a named user and an auditor will immediately flag diffused ownership as a critical failure because if nobody is specifically accountable for an asset then nobody is responsible when that asset is compromised meaning if a confidential legal document is left exposed on a printer tray or if an employee device is left unlocked on a desk collective responsibility guarantees zero individual accountability

This brings us directly to the question of asset valuation and which items actually mattered most to the organization because if an auditor walks into an administrative facility they want to see that the organization has prioritized its defenses around its most critical data assets rather than treating every piece of office furniture with equal weight since in our risk assessment we evaluated physical filing cabinets containing sensitive legal records and server enclosures housing organizational data alongside routine items like biometric attendance devices and shared office desks where while the biometric terminal in the lobby is important for daily operations an attack or failure there only impacts attendance tracking whereas the legal case records and the organizational server equipment represent core operational continuity and legal liability

Yet when you examine how our group and the agency treated these high impact assets a glaring disconnect emerges because for the server equipment and the confidential case records our risk evaluation yielded moderate risk scores yet the designated treatment in multiple scenarios was simply to accept the risk based on the physical perimeter security of the building relying on the core assumption that because the exterior doors were controlled and the equipment was located inside a designated room the risk was inherently managed but an auditor would tear that justification apart instantly because relying on a locked building door to protect internal servers and confidential case files is not a security control but rather an administrative wishful thinking exercise

An auditor demands objective evidence which is where the biggest gap in our original assessment becomes obvious because if an organization claims that access to server enclosures or legal folders is strictly restricted an auditor will ask for access control logs and visitor sign in sheets and keycard audit trails and periodic review records showing who has entered those spaces and when while also requesting active directory configuration exports and permission matrix documentation and periodic user access reviews if an organization claims employee computers are secure because folder permissions are properly configured to verify that terminated employees or unauthorized personnel do not retain active permissions

In our fieldwork observations none of that documentary evidence was actively verified because we trusted the observable physical state of the room at the exact moment we walked through it seeing a door closed and a filing cabinet sitting in a corner and writing down that the risk was low or moderately acceptable which means an auditor would immediately uncover the hidden gaps beneath that surface level observation and discover that policies regarding screen locking or document disposal exist only as unwritten cultural habits rather than enforced automated configurations meaning when employees step away from their workstations screens remain unlocked because there is no group policy object enforcing a five minute timeout and sensitive printed documents sit unattended near shared printers because printer release software or secure print authentication has not been implemented

Furthermore an auditor would investigate how the agency handles the end of life cycle for sensitive information since our assessment noted a lack of an accessible office shredder in the immediate workspace meaning sensitive physical documents had to be transported to another department or retained for extended periods before disposal which to an auditor means retaining sensitive physical documents without a strict and logged destruction schedule creates an unmonitored window of exposure because if an external auditor asks for proof of secure disposal and the organization can only reply that documents are kept in a filing cabinet until someone remembers to shred them that fails compliance standards immediately

Reflecting on these auditor realities completely changes how we should view the recommendations our group originally proposed because in our conclusion we emphasized shifting toward a defense in depth model and implementing data redundancy like raid configurations or erasure coding and conducting a business impact analysis to establish recovery time objectives and recovery point objectives while those technical recommendations are entirely sound from an information assurance perspective they are incomplete without the administrative foundation of an asset inventory and a strict audit checklist

An asset inventory ensures you actually know the exact footprint of what you are trying to defend by eliminating shadow devices and unaccounted workstations while an audit checklist provides the repeatable verification mechanism needed to prove that your defenses are not just theoretical ideas written in a student report but active and functioning barriers backed by logs and accountability so that when you tie technical treatments together with rigorous asset tracking and continuous audit validation you stop treating security as a reactive checkbox exercise and start building genuine operational resilience which is the ultimate lesson the eyes of an auditor teach us by reminding us that trust in a system is never given away freely based on a polite conversation but must always be continuously proven through hard evidence

Lessons I Learned from Information Security Risk Assessment Fieldwork

September 4, 2026

When you study Information Assurance and Security in a classroom setting, concepts like risk scores, impact ratings, single points of failure, and business continuity feel very clean and academic. You sit in front of a spreadsheet, multiply numbers together, and write down neat solutions. But step out of the textbook and walk into a real operational workplace to conduct actual risk assessment fieldwork, and reality hits you pretty quickly. Recently, our group had the opportunity to evaluate an organization’s physical and informational security environment. That fieldwork completely changed my perspective on how security works in practice versus how it looks on paper.

Going to the organization was actually a little nerve wracking for us. Even though we had our official request letter properly checked out and endorsed by our instructor, we were still pretty nervous just walking up and asking to enter and observe the place. As college students, stepping into a formal workplace to evaluate how they run things can feel intimidating. Obviously, I can't say anything direct about where we went or what specific organization it was, but it was really interesting to see the inner workings of an office environment, even if our observation wasn't deeply technical or intrusive. The interview part ended up being much lighter than we expected too, mostly because we personally knew the employee we spoke with. Because of that rapport, all of our questions were smooth, inline with our assessment form, and completely non invasive.

To respect the privacy and security of the organization, I won't be naming the entity or exposing specific internal vulnerabilities. Instead, I want to reflect on what our fieldwork taught us about the real world gaps between existing controls, human habits, and system dependability, and how our group reasoned through risk evaluation and treatment using the core concepts from our Information Assurance and Security class.

Context and Scope of the Fieldwork Assessment

Our assessment targeted key operational units responsible for day to day administrative tasks, legal documentation, and planning operations, alongside common areas used by employees and visitors every day. The main objective was to evaluate physical asset handling, record keeping practices, workstation security, and overall office environment risks. Going in, we knew that physical security and information security are deeply intertwined. A server running top tier software security is still vulnerable if the physical room hosting it isn't properly safeguarded, and confidential records are only as secure as the cabinet or desk holding them.

During our walkthroughs and discussions, we assessed ten distinct risk scenarios across administrative, legal, and planning environments. We tracked assets ranging from unlocked employee workstations, server enclosures, and physical filing cabinets to official printed documents, common biometric timekeeping devices, and office shredding setups.

How We Reasoned Through Risk Identification and Analysis

To analyze the data collected during our fieldwork, our group relied on a standard qualitative risk matrix. We evaluated each scenario by determining its Impact (ranging from Minor to Moderate) and its Probability (evaluating likelihood on a standardized weight scale). Multiplying these factors gave us a quantitative Risk Score, categorized into Low and Moderate risk levels.

Across the ten scenarios we evaluated, eight came out as Moderate risk scores, while two were rated as Low risk. Interestingly, every single risk scenario shared a baseline probability of being "Possible." This uniform likelihood highlighted an important realization for us: small operational lapses happen constantly in a busy working environment. Whether it is an employee walking away from a desktop without locking the screen or a confidential file sitting on a printer tray, these aren't rare anomalies, they are everyday tendencies.

What struck us most during the evaluation process was how heavily the organization relied on physical perimeter controls. For 80% of the identified risks, the initial management strategy was simply to "Accept" the risk. The primary reasoning was that because entry into the building or specific office suites was controlled, internal assets were considered inherently safe. However, as students analyzing this through an information assurance lens, relying on perimeter security as a single point of failure felt like a major vulnerability.

Bridging Classroom Lessons with Fieldwork Insights

Our coursework emphasizes that system dependability is not an assumed trait; it is the trustworthiness of a computing system that allows reliance to be justifiably placed on the service it delivers. As noted in recent literature on system reliability, dependability is not a single property but a cluster of them, including reliability, availability, safety, integrity, and maintainability (Djambazova, 2026). Above all, dependability must be a design decision made early in the lifecycle, not a band aid repair applied late when something breaks.

During our fieldwork, we noticed that many existing controls treated security as an afterthought. For instance, accepting the risk of unlocked employee computers or shared workstations because "the office door is restricted" ignores the principles of defense in depth and insider threat management. If an unauthorized person passes the entrance, or if an internal user misbehaves, there are zero secondary barriers to protect sensitive data on unattended screens.

Another major theme from our fieldwork was the handling of physical records. Relying solely on single physical copies of crucial case records or planning documents stored on high shelves or unlocked cabinets introduces severe single points of failure. If a physical disaster occurs, such as a fire, water leak, or theft, the availability and integrity of that information are instantly destroyed. Protecting information requires looking beyond the physical room holding the paper and focusing on data redundancy.

Refining Recommendations from an IAS Student Perspective

When formulating recommendations, our group had to ground our thinking in what we learned as Information Assurance and Security students rather than making administrative demands. We realized that security recommendations shouldn't just be about buying expensive gear or waiting for top management deadlines; they should focus on smart, structural improvements that build resilience directly into daily operations.

First, we recommended establishing layered digital controls to support physical perimeter security. Simple, zero cost policy adjustments, like enforcing automated 5 minute screen lock policies across all workstations and requiring individual user accounts on shared office machines, ensure clear audit trails and reduce reliance on manual user discipline.

Second, to address physical records management, we recommended implementing robust data redundancy practices. Important physical documents should be systematically digitized. For on premises digital storage, implementing data replication or RAID configurations on server equipment ensures high availability and fault tolerance. As digital volume grows, organizations can also adopt erasure coding for cloud or distributed backups, which encodes and distributes data across nodes to maintain fault tolerance while cutting down on the heavy storage overhead required by pure replication (Cui & Wang, 2025).

Finally, our fieldwork highlighted the necessity of Business Continuity Management (BCM). Business continuity is advance planning, nobody writes a good recovery plan while a crisis is actively unfolding. By conducting a Business Impact Analysis (BIA) to identify time sensitive operations, the organization can establish clear Recovery Time Objectives (RTO) and Recovery Point Objectives (RPO). Connecting technology continuity, emergency response, and operational resilience into one integrated system ensures that critical operations remain functional even during unexpected disruptions.

References

Cui, L., & Wang, X. (2025). Efficient erasure coding strategies for distributed cloud storage architectures. Journal of Systems Architecture and Security, 148, 103–115.

Djambazova, M. (2026). Frameworks for system dependability and adjustable reliability in enterprise architectures. Academic Press.

Reflection on the Security Vulnerabilities of the Student Made Platform

August 3, 2026

Honestly, I can see myself making these mistakes. You are 48 hours into a weekend coding. You just want the app to work before the judges walk by. So you prompt ChatGPT or Claude, it spews out 200 lines of pristine looking JavaScript, you hit copy paste and boom the button works, the server returns 200 OK. You win a minor prize, collect a tiny gift card and go home to sleep. End of story, right? Except for three students at a local uni here it was not the end. It was the start of a total security trainwreck.

What started as a 72 hour hackathon project for student orgs quietly turned into actual production software. Within a month, over a dozen campus groups were using it. And without anyone actually deciding to launch, this fragile little app was suddenly holding real, sensitive data like full names, student numbers, phone numbers, GCash payment screenshots, uploaded ID photos, and private chats. Then a student tried changing the number in the URL, moving from profile 1048 to profile 1049 and was instantly staring at a complete stranger's profile, contact info, and uploaded ID.

Look, as a 3rd year student who is still trying to figure out backend dev myself, I really feel for them. They were not trying to be shady, they were just young devs trying to solve a real campus problem fast. But, looking at how it was set up under the hood, it was a complete technical disaster waiting to happen that could actually harm people.

What Went Wrong

If you have ever let an AI write your backend code without double checking it, you already know what happened here. AI is awesome at giving you stuff that runs, but it is pretty terrible at writing stuff that is secure unless you specifically ask it to.

They just wanted the app to run, and in the process, they broke some core secure design principles we keep hearing about in class.

1. The URL trick, or Insecure Direct Object References

Changing the ID in the URL showed someone else's account. This violated the principles of Fail Safe Defaults and Complete Mediation. The AI wrote a basic route that checked if you were logged in, but completely forgot to check who was logged in. It just assumed that if you typed profile 1049, you were allowed to see it. It defaulted to letting everyone in instead of blocking by default, which breaks Fail Safe Defaults, and it did not double check permissions on the request, which breaks Complete Mediation.

2. DB credentials sitting on public GitHub

The database password and payment API key were literally in plain text in an early commit. This violated the principle of Open Design. Hardcoding keys locally during a hackathon happens to all of us, but pushing them to GitHub is an oof moment. Open Design basically says your app should not rely on people not finding your source code to stay safe, because eventually someone will find it.

3. God mode DB access and no HTTPS

The app was running over plain HTTP and using a database user that had full admin permissions. This violated Least Privilege and Defense in Depth. Running plain HTTP on a college Wi-Fi network is scary because anyone sniffing traffic can see everything. Plus, giving the backend full admin access to the database means if one part breaks, the attacker gets access to the whole database instead of just what that route needs, which breaks Least Privilege.

4. Raw error traces on screen

Triggering a backend crash showed full code stack traces on screen, which violated Fail Safe Defaults. When an error happens, showing raw server code on the screen gives potential hackers a free blueprint of how your server is set up. When a faculty adviser was shown the URL trick, the app was pulled offline that afternoon. Luckily, no malicious breach seems to have happened, but the exposure was super scary.

How I Would Try to Fix This as a 3rd Year

If I were asked to help fix this, I would split it into quick emergency fixes and then better long term changes once we have time to study it properly.

Phase 1: Emergency containment
First off, keep the app offline. Do not rush to put it back up. Assume every secret pushed to GitHub is leaked, so change the database passwords, reset payment API keys, and log out all active user sessions. Making the repo private is not enough because the keys are still in the commit history, so we would need to use tools like git filter repo or just start a clean repo after wiping secrets to strip them out completely, which restores Open Design. Add a simple if check in the backend to make sure the logged in user ID actually matches the profile ID being requested before sending back data, which restores Complete Mediation. Finally, set up free SSL through Cloudflare or Let's Encrypt so data is not traveling in plain text across campus Wi-Fi, which restores Defense in Depth.

Phase 2: Long term fixes when we have time
Once the panic dies down, we would need to redo the architecture a bit. Instead of profile 1048, we should use random string IDs like UUIDs so people cannot just guess numbers in the URL. Student IDs and GCash receipts should not sit in a public images folder, so they should be stored in a private cloud bucket, and the app should only generate temporary 5 minute links when an authorized user asks to view them. Lastly, create a separate database user that can only read or write specific tables so it does not have full admin rights over everything, which restores Least Privilege.

Rushing Isn’t Responsible and RA 10173

Building stuff fast with AI is super cool, but there is a big difference between a project you show off at a weekend hackathon and an app holding real people's private data. When you ask AI for backend code, it gives you whatever works quickest. It does not care about security unless you specifically ask for it. If we just copy, paste, and deploy without checking, that is on us as developers.

Also, we have to remember our legal responsibilities under RA No. 10173, or the Data Privacy Act of 2012. Student numbers, phone numbers, and uploaded IDs are sensitive personal information. Under Section 20 of the DPA, anyone building apps that collect this data is legally required to put proper security measures in place. If an actual breach happened here, the uni and the developers could have faced serious trouble with the National Privacy Commission.

It is really sad because these students had awesome initiative and built something people actually wanted to use. But shipping fast cannot be an excuse when real people trust us with their privacy. We definitely need to learn to slow down and check our code before hitting deploy.
